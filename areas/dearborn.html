Lead Paint Abatement in Dearborn, MI

Dearborn is known for Ford and for postwar subdivisions, but its older neighborhoods hold housing from the early automobile era. A remodel or home purchase in one of those houses should start by learning which painted surfaces contain lead.

Older cores inside a growing suburb

The Detroit Historical Society covers Dearborn in its Wayne County history project on cities shaped by the expansion of the automobile industry. Growth came in waves, so one block can hold 1920s housing while the next is postwar.

That mix makes the construction date of the specific house important. Additions and renovations can hide original painted components, and the age of the neighborhood does not answer the age of every surface.

What that means for lead

Before opening walls during a kitchen or bathroom remodel, identify which painted surfaces will be disturbed. A home’s appearance does not establish the age of every addition or paint layer. Bring building records and any earlier lead report to the conversation.

A Dearborn remodel needs its own project scope, not a Detroit rental-certificate checklist. For a paid renovation in applicable older housing, ask about EPA lead-safe renovation qualifications. If the purpose is permanent control of an identified hazard, discuss Michigan abatement qualifications instead.

Common questions

I am buying a 1940s Dearborn house. Is a general home inspection enough?

A general inspection does not identify lead paint surface by surface. A lead-based paint inspection uses XRF or lab analysis and gives you a room-by-room record before you remodel.

The house was remodeled once already. Does that remove the risk?

Not by itself. New finishes can cover lead paint without removing it. Ask what the earlier work disturbed, and test the surfaces your project will touch.
