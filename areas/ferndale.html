Ferndale grew along Woodward Avenue with the streetcar and the nearby Ford plant, and its older streets are full of early twentieth-century houses with original trim, doors and windows. Those moving painted parts are where lead dust usually starts.

A streetcar suburb with early housing

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation describes Ferndale as a community linked to Woodward Avenue, the nearby Ford plant in Highland Park and suburban development. Housing from that era has now been painted and repainted for a century.

Sticking sashes and painted doorways grind coatings into fine dust a little at a time. The damage is easy to miss because it rarely looks like dramatic peeling.

What that means for lead

A sticking sash or painted doorway can produce friction dust without a dramatic patch of peeling wall paint. Describe the part that rubs, where dust collects and whether it returns after cleaning. That information is more useful than asking for generic “toxic paint removal.”

In a Ferndale home, the work might involve replacement of a component, a renovation with lead-safe practices or an abatement plan. Preserve any original-feature concerns in the proposal. Ask which pieces remain in place and how the surrounding room will be protected during removal.

Common questions

There is no peeling paint, just dust that keeps coming back. Is that a lead issue?

It can be. Friction between painted parts makes fine dust without obvious paint failure. Describe which parts rub and where the dust collects, and ask about a risk assessment or dust testing.

I want to keep my original trim. Does that rule out lead work?

No. The proposal should say which components stay and how the room is protected while work happens. Preserving original features and controlling lead can be planned together.
